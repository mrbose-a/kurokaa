# Would your kitchen survive this Tuesday?

*The unknown flow of food safety in Kolkata's kitchens, why it is about to matter more than usual, and what we are doing about it.*

*A 10-minute read from Kurokaa. Published ahead of Pujo 2026.*

---

There is a question every restaurant owner in Kolkata can answer instantly, and one almost none of them can.

The first: *how did we do last night?* Covers, revenue, what sold, who was late. You know it before you have finished your first cup of tea.

The second: *if a food safety officer had walked in at half past seven last night, in the middle of service, would we have passed?*

Not "are our papers in order." Not "did we renew the licence." Would the kitchen, as it was actually running at that moment, with the fridge that has been reading a little warm since July and the temperature log that the night cook fills in from memory, have withstood a stranger with a probe thermometer and a notice pad?

Most owners do not know. And the honest ones will tell you they do not know. That gap between what a kitchen believes about itself and what it can prove about itself is the subject of this piece. It is a gap we have watched from both sides of the pass for more than a decade, and it is the gap we have built a practice to close.

## The flow nobody drew

Every kitchen has a food safety system. Very few have ever seen it.

Food arrives at a delivery door. It is checked, or it is not. It goes into a dry store, a chiller, a freezer. It is dated, or it is not. It is prepped, cooked, held, plated, served, and what is left is cooled or thrown away. At every one of those joins, something is supposed to happen: a temperature taken, a label written, a surface cleaned, a record signed. Indian law, through the Food Safety and Standards Act and Schedule 4 of the 2011 Regulations, describes a good deal of this in writing.

But in the kitchen itself, none of it is written anywhere a cook can see. It lives in habit. The senior guy knows the chicken goes on the bottom shelf. The new guy does not, and nobody told him, because nobody remembers being told either. The cleaning schedule exists in the owner's head and the register lives in a drawer the health inspector will open before the owner does.

This is what we mean by the *unknown flow*. Not that the kitchen is dirty. Most are not. It is that the kitchen cannot describe, in sequence and on paper, what happens to a piece of food between the delivery door and the plate, and therefore cannot show that each step was done. Food safety in India is, in practice, a due diligence system. What you did matters. What you can prove you did is what decides whether you keep your doors open.

## Why the failure is almost never exotic

When people picture a food safety failure they picture something dramatic: rats, rot, a poisoning. The reality, in our experience, is embarrassingly ordinary.

It is a fridge nobody probed, because the display on the door says four degrees and nobody thought to check whether the display was telling the truth. It is a probe thermometer that reads three degrees warm and has read three degrees warm for months, which means every temperature log in the building was written in good faith and is wrong. It is a water test nobody booked. It is a pest control report from a company that stopped coming in March. It is a training record that exists for the head chef and for nobody else.

None of these is expensive to fix. A probe is a few hundred rupees. A water test is a phone call. A cleaning schedule is a sheet of paper and a pen on a string. The reason they go unfixed is not money. It is that they are invisible from the inside. The chef is too close to the room to see it. The owner is looking at the P&L. And the first person who walks the kitchen with a written standard in their hand and tells the truth about it is, far too often, the one holding the notice.

## What it actually costs

Enforcement in Indian cities arrives unannounced. That is not a flaw in the system; it is the entire point of it. A scheduled inspection measures how well you tidy up. An unannounced one measures how you actually run.

When it goes wrong, it goes wrong in a specific order. Licences can be suspended under section 32 of the Act. Spot fines are levied. Stock is seized on the day. And a suspension is not a fine you absorb and move on from. It is shutters down, staff idle, rent still due, and a queue of regulars finding somewhere else to eat while you sort it out.

The repair usually takes a week. The photograph of your shutter with a notice on it lasts as long as the internet does.

We want to be careful here, because fear is a cheap way to sell things and we are not interested in selling that way. So let us be precise about the risk. The risk is not that your kitchen is dangerous. The risk is that your kitchen is *undocumented*, and an undocumented kitchen has no way to demonstrate the care it takes. In a due diligence system, that is the same thing as not taking it.

## Why now, and why Kolkata

Pujo is a few weeks out.

For a food business in this city that means the busiest fortnight of the year, temporary staff who have never seen your kitchen, suppliers stretched thin, storage overflowing, and a run of days when service starts at noon and ends when the pandal lights go off. Everything that is marginal in September becomes critical in October. The fridge that is coping now will not cope with double the stock. The record that gets filled in most days will not get filled in at all.

And it is the season when enforcement tends to be most active, because the authorities know what we know: that this is when kitchens are under the most strain, and when the public is eating out in the greatest numbers. A busy Pujo season is a good thing for everyone. It is also exactly when a kitchen is most likely to be walked, and least likely to be ready.

We have been hearing the same worry from owners across the city. Not "we are doing something wrong." More often, "we do not know what we would be caught on, and we do not have anyone to ask who will tell us the truth."

That is the problem we set out to solve.

## What "everyone wins" actually means

Before we describe what we do, it is worth saying why we do it, because the why shapes the how.

We do not think food safety is a tax on restaurants. We think it is the one part of the business where the interests of every party line up perfectly, if anyone bothers to look.

The owner wins, because a kitchen that can prove its own diligence does not lose a fortnight of Pujo trade to a suspension, and does not spend the rest of the year explaining a photograph.

The chef wins, because a written standard is a shield. The cook who was told the chicken goes on the bottom shelf, and can point to where that is written, is protected in a way that the cook who was "supposed to know" never is.

The customer wins, obviously, and the customer never finds out. That is the point. The best food safety work is invisible to the person eating.

And the regulator wins, because the regulator's job is not to close restaurants. It is to have restaurants that do not need closing. Every kitchen that can demonstrate a working system is one fewer the food safety department has to worry about. A city where kitchens fix their fridges before the inspector arrives is a city where the inspector's job is easier and the public is safer. Nobody in government is unhappy about that outcome. It is the outcome the whole system was designed to produce.

So when we say we do this work patiently, and with the sole intention that everyone should win, we mean it literally. There is no version of a properly run kitchen where somebody loses. Our job is simply to get kitchens there before somebody else gets there first.

Here is how a review works.

You agree a window, say a fortnight. We arrive inside it, without telling you when. We introduce ourselves with an engagement letter and photo ID, and if nobody is there who can authorise the visit, we wait or we come back. We never walk a kitchen on someone else's say-so.

Then we walk it the way food does: receiving, dry store, chilled, frozen, prep, cook, hold, service, wash-up, waste. Forty-four controls across fifteen areas, in flow order, because the failures live in the joins between sections. Every unit is probed, temperatures taken into a food item and not read off a door display, and then we test your probe against ours in iced water. We read thirty days of records, looking for the gaps and for the too-perfect. We have two short conversations with your team, not a test and nothing attributed, to find out whether the system lives in your people or only on paper.

Anything critical, you hear out loud before we leave. Nothing urgent waits for a document.

Within seventy-two hours you have a written report: a plain-language answer to whether this kitchen would withstand an inspection today, every finding graded critical, major, minor or observation with a deadline, dated photographs, and a thirty-day plan that orders the work by what is cheapest and fastest to put right. And a section most reports leave out: what we could not assess, and why. A report that claims to have seen everything is not thorough. It is exaggerating.

## What we are not

We would rather lose a booking than have you misunderstand this, so in plain terms.

The Pass is not an FSSAI-recognised food safety auditing agency. A review is not a regulatory audit, it does not reduce how often you are inspected, and it certifies nothing. There is no score, no grade, no stars, because a number that resembles a hygiene rating invites you to treat it as one, and that scheme belongs to a regulator, not to us. Anyone telling you otherwise about their own service is worth a second look.

What it is: an honest, unannounced look at your kitchen by someone who has run one, with every limit in the report traced back to where it came from. Regulatory references are cited to a named Indian instrument. Our own operational defaults, drawn from UK practice, are marked as ours and carry no regulatory claim at all. A rule you choose to adopt that is stricter than ours is marked as yours. Nobody's number quietly becomes "an FSSAI requirement" because it once appeared in a report.

And one more thing, in writing, because The Pass is run by a company that builds restaurant software: your report will never recommend Kurokaa. Findings say what is wrong and what to do about it, and a paper record signed twice a day satisfies every one of them. If you later want software, that is a conversation you start. It is never a finding we write.

## What happens to what we see

You are letting a stranger photograph your kitchen and talk to your staff, so you should know exactly where all of it goes.

The report goes to you and nobody else, not a regulator, not a landlord, not an insurer, unless you ask us in writing to send it. Photographs are of findings, not of people; faces are obscured, and we photograph a drain or a shelf, never a member of your team at work. Your staff are never named. No case studies without your written permission, asked for properly, afterwards, and easy to refuse. We keep the file for two years so a re-visit has something to compare against, then delete it, or sooner if you ask.

The one thing we reserve is the right to end an engagement if something critical is found and simply left. We are not inspectors and we will not report you to anyone. But we will not stay on as the name attached to a kitchen that has decided to ignore us.

## The longer road

The review solves the immediate problem: knowing, before Pujo, where you stand and what to fix first. That is the urgent thing and it is why we are writing this now.

The longer problem is keeping it that way. A kitchen that is right in October drifts by January unless the standard lives somewhere more durable than habit. That is the work Kurokaa exists to do: a digital evidence trail and standardised operations, so that the answer to "could we survive Tuesday" stays current instead of going stale the month after we leave. But that is a separate conversation, and it comes second. You cannot standardise a flow you have never seen. First, someone has to walk the room and tell you the truth about it.
