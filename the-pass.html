The gap

Nobody calls ahead.

Enforcement in Indian cities arrives unannounced — that is the entire point of it. Licences are suspended under section 32 of the Food Safety and Standards Act, spot fines are levied, and stock is seized on the day. The kitchen does not get a week's notice to tidy up.

Which means the only honest question is not are your records in order for the inspection? It is would you survive one this Tuesday, at half past seven, in the middle of service, with the fridge you have been meaning to fix?

Most kitchens genuinely do not know. Not because anyone is careless — because nobody has ever walked the room with a written standard in their hand and told them the truth. The chef is too close to it. The owner is looking at the P&L. And the first person to look properly is usually holding a notice pad.

The review

Ninety minutes, unannounced.

You agree the window — a fortnight, say — and we arrive inside it without telling you when. A scheduled review measures how well you tidy up. An unannounced one measures how you actually run.

0:00

We arrive and introduce ourselves

Engagement letter in hand and photo ID. If nobody is there who can authorise the visit, we wait or we come back. We never walk a kitchen on someone else's say-so.

0:05

Delivery door to plate, in that order

Receiving, dry store, chilled, frozen, prep, cook, hold, service, wash-up, waste. Food moves in a sequence and so do we, because the failures live in the joins between sections.

0:45

Every unit probed, and your probe checked too

Temperatures taken into a food item, not read off a door display. Then we test your probe against ours in iced water. A probe reading three degrees warm means months of records written in good faith and wrong.

0:57

Thirty days of records

Temperature logs, cleaning schedule, pest reports, water test, licence, training records, oil disposal. We look for the gaps — and for the too-perfect.

1:12

Two conversations with your team

Not a test, and nobody is reported for an answer. We ask one chef and one other person what they would do if a fridge read nine degrees. It tells us whether the system lives in your people or only on paper.

1:22

You hear the serious findings before we leave

Anything critical is said out loud, to you or your head chef, on the day. Nothing urgent waits for a document to be typed up.

What lands in your inbox

A written report within seventy-two hours: a plain-language answer to whether this kitchen would withstand an inspection today, every finding graded critical, major, minor or observation with a deadline attached, dated photographs, and a thirty-day plan that orders the work by what is cheapest and fastest to put right.

And a section most reports leave out — what we could not assess, and why. The freezer that was mid-defrost, the file the manager could not find. A report claiming to have seen everything is not thorough, it is exaggerating.

What happens to what we see

You are letting a stranger photograph your kitchen and talk to your staff, so you should know exactly where all of it goes before you agree to anything.

  • The report goes to you. Nobody else receives it — not a regulator, not a landlord, not an insurer — unless you ask us in writing to send it.
  • Photographs are of findings, not of people. Faces are obscured. We photograph a drain or a shelf, never a member of your team at work.
  • Your staff are never named. The two conversations we have are to understand whether the system works, not to identify who does not follow it. Nothing anyone says is attributed.
  • No case studies without your written permission — asked for properly, afterwards, and easy to refuse. Your kitchen will not appear on this website because you once hired us.
  • We keep the file for two years so a later re-visit has something to compare against, then delete it. Ask us to delete it sooner and we will.

The one thing we reserve is the right to end the engagement if something critical is found and simply left. We are not inspectors and we will not report you to anyone — but we will not stay on as the name attached to a kitchen that has decided to ignore us.

The trail

Every limit says where it came from.

A consultant's number that quietly becomes “an FSSAI requirement” because it once appeared in a report is how people get misled without anyone deciding to mislead them. So every control we check carries its own source.

  • Regulatory reference. Traceable to a named Indian instrument — the Food Safety and Standards Act or Schedule 4 of the 2011 Regulations. Cited in the report, and re-checked annually.
  • Operational default. Our own working rule, drawn from UK kitchen practice. Marked as ours, and carrying no regulatory claim at all. Most of the control set sits here, honestly.
  • Your own standard. A rule stricter than ours that you choose to adopt. Set with you during the review, never imposed.

Forty-four controls across fifteen areas — receiving, chilled and frozen storage, cooking, hot holding, cooling, cross-contamination, allergens, people, pests, water, ice, cleaning, waste, records and the building itself. Each one has a limit you can measure, a frequency, and a corrective action.

There is no score, no grade and no stars. A percentage would be easier to sell and it would be the wrong thing to sell — a number that resembles a hygiene rating invites you to treat it as one, and that scheme belongs to a regulator, not to us. You get counts by severity and a judgement in plain words.

What we are not

The Pass is not an FSSAI-recognised food safety auditing agency. This review is not a regulatory audit, it does not reduce how often you are inspected, and it certifies nothing. Anyone telling you otherwise about their own service is worth a second look.

What it is: an independent, unannounced operational review by a chef, against a written standard, producing a report you can act on — and, because we hold no official status, nothing stopping us from telling you exactly what we found.

And who we are

The Pass is the standards practice of Kurokaa, which builds restaurant management software. You should know that before you hire us, not after.

So, in writing: your report will never recommend Kurokaa. Findings say what is wrong and what to do about it in plain terms, and a paper record signed twice a day satisfies every one of them. If you later want software, that is a separate conversation you start — never a finding we write.
